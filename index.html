01 — Overview

Breaking the silence around menstrual health

LunaCare began as an observation: most women's health applications approach menstrual wellness through clinical language and sterile data interfaces — creating barriers to the very conversations they're meant to enable.

I designed a conversational interface that uses progressive disclosure, empathetic microcopy, and HCI-informed interaction patterns to reduce cognitive load while surfacing the right information at the right moment.

02 — Problem

Health information shouldn't feel clinical

Women seeking menstrual health information navigate a fragmented ecosystem: medical websites use clinical jargon, social media spreads misinformation, and dedicated apps focus on tracking without education.

The core HCI challenge: how do you present sensitive, nuanced health information in a way that feels approachable — without sacrificing accuracy or depth?

  • 01
    Users aged 11–15 consistently rated existing apps as "confusing" or "scary" in preliminary interviews
  • 02
    Information architecture in competitor apps was designed around medical workflows, not user mental models
  • 03
    Zero applications in the market combined conversational AI with peer-community support effectively
03 — Research

Research Insights

I conducted contextual interviews with 8 participants across the target age range (11–40), supplemented by competitive analysis of 6 existing health platforms.

User journey mapping revealed three critical pain points: discovery friction, trust barriers, and information overload in initial onboarding.

  • ↑
    Conversational tone rated 3.2× more trustworthy than formal clinical language across all age groups
  • →
    Progressive disclosure reduced onboarding drop-off by presenting one decision at a time
  • ✓
    Community features increased return visits by creating social accountability
04 — Wireframes

Low-fidelity to high-fidelity

I iterated through three rounds of wireframes, testing each with 3–5 users. Key design decisions emerged around chatbot placement, quick-reply patterns, and content hierarchy.

05 — Design

Final Design System

The final interface uses a soft, warm palette to reduce clinical associations. The chatbot employs progressive disclosure — starting with quick-reply options before opening full text input — to lower the barrier for younger users.

Key components: a floating chat widget, education hub with categorised articles, and a community section with peer stories. All designed for mobile-first with WCAG 2.1 AA compliance.

07 — Reflection

What I would do differently

LunaCare taught me that designing for sensitive health topics requires an extra layer of empathy at every design decision — from the microcopy in error states to the information hierarchy on landing screens.

If I were to iterate further, I would invest more in co-design sessions with users from the younger end of the age range (11–14), as their mental models differ significantly from adult users. I would also explore richer data visualisation for cycle tracking that doesn't reduce health to numbers.

The project solidified my interest in HCI at the intersection of health and accessibility — themes I hope to explore further at masters level in Germany.

Overview

Peer support for underserved conversations

FemCare Community 2.0 emerged from the insight that women's health discourse is often siloed — clinical sources lack warmth, peer networks lack accuracy. This project combined both.

I led the end-to-end UX process: user journey mapping, information architecture, and empathy-driven interaction design. I also built and deployed the community SPA, iterating on features through structured usability testing.

Key UX Decisions

Design rationale

  • IA
    Flat information architecture — no more than 2 taps to any resource — to serve users with limited digital literacy
  • JM
    User journey mapping identified three primary user archetypes with distinct information needs and access patterns
  • UT
    Three rounds of usability testing with structured feedback protocols informed each design iteration
Reflection

Learning through iteration

This project established my usability testing methodology. The discipline of structured feedback rounds — defining tasks, observing without intervening, measuring completion rates — is now central to my design process.

Grievance Portal Redesign

A civic complaint management portal designed to streamline grievance resolution for government stakeholders through end-to-end interaction design.

Duration
Jul 2024 – Aug 2025
Role
UX Designer
Focus
Accessibility, Multi-device
Output

Overview

Making civic processes human

Government grievance systems are often designed from the back-end out — optimised for administrative processing rather than citizen comprehension. This redesign reversed the lens.

I developed end-to-end interaction flows, wireframes, and high-fidelity prototypes for the complaint submission pipeline, then conducted multi-device usability testing to refine the interface.

Key Contributions

Design decisions

  • 01
    Redesigned the submission flow from a single dense form to a guided multi-step process with clear progress indication
  • 02
    Multi-device usability testing revealed 3 critical interaction failures in the original design that were fixed through iteration
  • 03
    Accessibility audit identified WCAG violations; all were resolved in the final prototype
